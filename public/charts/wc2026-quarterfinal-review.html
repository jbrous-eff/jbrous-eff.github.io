xG

Expected goals, in one breath

Every shot gets a probability of scoring based on where and how it was taken. Add them up and you get xG — a measure of the chances a team created, stripped of luck. A team that wins 2–0 on 3.04–0.14 xG (France, QF1) earned every bit of it.

The models

Six forecasters live on this page: the launch Poisson blend (Model A) plus a five-model ensemble — dynamic state-space, tournament form, Elo, and a club-form prior, weighted by measured backtest skill. Every forecast was published before its match. The scoreboard is in the Forecast Lab.

The story

Spain have played seven matches at this World Cup and conceded one goal — De Ketelaere's, in the quarterfinal — with six clean sheets, the first team ever to keep six at a single World Cup. They are unbeaten in 37 straight matches, have won 8 consecutive knockout games at major tournaments, and just held the French front four that terrorized everyone to 0.31 xG in a semifinal that was never close. The reigning European champions have been the best team in this tournament from the opening week, and it isn't particularly arguable.

Argentina's counter-argument is not an argument; it's a fact pattern. A tournament-high 19 goals, 12 of them after the 75th minute. Two knockout wins secured by goals in second-half stoppage time — no team had ever done that twice in one World Cup. Fourteen consecutive wins, 19 straight major-tournament matches unbeaten, and a 39-year-old captain with 8 goals and 4 assists — 33 career World Cup goal contributions, the all-time record — playing, by his own account, the last World Cup match of his life. They have trailed in the 75th minute or later in three knockout games this month. They won all three.

Tactically it's the cleanest contrast a final has offered in years: control against chaos. Both teams are expected at full strength in mirrored 4–3–3s — Rodri, Fabián Ruiz and Olmo trying to strangle the midfield Argentina likes to flood; Oyarzabal ahead of Yamal and Baena against the tournament's stingiest back line by xG; Messi central between Álvarez and Simeone, hunting the one transition Spain's press concedes per half. The stakes ledger, for history: Argentina are one win from a fourth consecutive major title (no nation has done it); Spain are one win from holding the men's and women's World Cups simultaneously (no nation has done that either). Something unprecedented happens at MetLife regardless.

The story

Fourteen years of history compressed into one fixture: the 2022 semifinal, replayed in Foxborough — except Morocco is no longer the team that defended its way to fourth place. At Qatar 2022 they held 38.8% possession and threw bodies in front of everything. At AFCON 2023 that number jumped to 54.9%. This tournament it's 60.4% — functionally identical to France's 60.5%. Walid Regragui's successors didn't just keep the wall; they learned to hold the ball. This is a genuine stylistic mirror match now, and the event data saw the transformation coming three years ago.

France, meanwhile, are the tournament's steamroller: 14 goals, a tournament-best 17.8 shots per game, 10.7 xG, and a front four — Mbappé, Dembélé, Olise, Barcola — that Norway's coach flatly called "the best in the competition by far." The one crack: Aurélien Tchouaméni's adductor is a game-time question, and Morocco's biggest blow is bigger — breakout star Ismael Saibari (3 goals, new €50m Bayern signing) is out with a hamstring injury. Morocco's false-nine system was built around him.

The story

Spain have not conceded a goal at this World Cup. Not one. Unai Simón's shutout streak stands at 609 minutes — a men's World Cup record, past the 517 of Italy 2006. And it's not keeper heroics: this is the same suffocation machine our Euro 2024 event data measured winning that tournament with a PPDA of 10.9 (passes allowed per defensive action — lower = more aggressive) and the highest press line in the field (65.7). They smother you where you build.

Belgium are the opposite: gloriously, chaotically alive. Down 2–0 to Senegal with 20 minutes left — won 3–2 in the 125th minute. Benched De Bruyne and Doku in the round of 16 and hammered the USA 4–1 anyway, with Charles De Ketelaere (2G+1A) looking like the heir the golden generation never got. But the ledger is grim: Amadou Onana's ACL rupture removes their only true midfield destroyer, and Spain's whole game is midfield asphyxiation. The last time these teams met in a World Cup knockout — 1986 — Belgium won on penalties. Belgium have never lost to Spain at a World Cup. That's the thread they're hanging by.

Quarterfinal 3 · AFTER EXTRA TIME · Hard Rock Stadium, Miami Gardens FL · preview below preserved as written

The Fairy Tale Against the Machine

After extra time — Norway 1, England 2 Schjelderup 36′, Bellingham 45+2′ and 93′ — the winner three minutes into extra time, pouncing on a Nyland error from close range. The fairy tale fought: Heggem had a second Norway goal disallowed on review for a Haaland shove before the corner, and it took 120 minutes to put the story down. But Haaland was silenced for the first time all tournament — subbed off late, his 14-match international scoring streak dead, his xG share strangled by a back line that gave Norway just 0.68 xG in two hours. England's first semifinal since 2018, on a Bellingham brace, at 66.8% ensemble confidence — the chorus of six models was right, but the game was every bit as violent as the 3.02 combined-xG projection promised.

The story

Norway's men had played one knockout match in their entire World Cup history — 1938, a 2–1 extra-time loss to Italy — before this summer. Now they've won two in twelve days, the second of them eliminating Brazil, with Erling Haaland scoring in the 79th and 90th minutes. It is Norway's first men's quarterfinal ever, their first World Cup at all since 1998 (a tournament where, incidentally, they also beat Brazil — the men are still unbeaten against them). Haaland has 7 goals on 3.7 xG: the most violent finishing overperformance in the field, from a man who does this every week in the Premier League.

England under Thomas Tuchel are the round's stylistic inverse: 65% possession, 15.6 shots a game, and the knockout resilience they historically lacked — they beat Mexico at the Azteca, with ten men, on Bellingham's two goals in 98 seconds. But the back line is patched: Quansah is suspended, Reece James is a hamstring doubt, Saka's Achilles is being managed. And Norway put at least one goal past everyone, including France. Both teams have scored and conceded in every Norway match this tournament. The nil-nil is nearly off the board (4.9%).

The Champion, the Clock, and the Wall of Switzerland

After extra time — Argentina 3, Switzerland 1 Mac Allister 10′ (heading in a Messi corner), Ndoye 67′, Álvarez 112′, Lautaro 120+1′. The tournament-form model's coin-flip warning aged beautifully: Switzerland equalized, dragged the champions past the 90th minute for the second straight round, and were within half an hour of penalties and Kobel-vs-Dibu — until Embolo's second yellow for simulation (72′) tilted the extra time that Álvarez's curler eventually settled. The strangest line of the night: Messi didn't score — his record streak of nine consecutive World Cup games with a goal, over. He supplied the corner instead. xG 2.00–0.53; the ensemble said 66.7%, Elo's 77.4% was closest, and 1–0-at-90′ was in fact the score at 90′... in the other direction of comfortable.

The story

Lionel Messi is 39 years old, playing his sixth World Cup, and leading the Golden Boot with 8 goals. Against Egypt he missed a penalty, watched his team fall 2–0 behind, then delivered the cross for Romero, scored the equalizer himself, and watched Enzo Fernández win it in stoppage time — the first team in World Cup history to trail by two in the 75th minute of a knockout and win in regulation. He has now scored in six consecutive World Cup knockout matches, which no one had ever done. Every match may be his last. He keeps refusing to let it be.

Switzerland's counter-argument is 132 years of patience cashing in at once: first knockout win in 88 years (2–0 over Algeria), then their first shootout survival (4–3 over Colombia, Kobel's save the difference), and a defense that has not trailed for a single minute all tournament. Xhaka, at his fourth World Cup, is now Switzerland's most-capped player ever. The blueprint is obvious — 0–0 as deep as possible; they generated 0.35 xG against Colombia and celebrated like it was a plan, because it was. One problem: penalties mean Dibu Martínez, and nobody on earth wants penalties against Dibu Martínez.

The Forecast Lab v2 · methods borrowed from other industries · six matches scored, one to go

Five Models Walk Into a Stadium

The launch-day model (call it Model A) was one equation. This is a real ensemble — four more forecasters, each imported from a field that isn't soccer, combined the way meteorologists combine weather models: weighted by measured skill on past tournaments, not by which one sounds cleverest. Every knockout forecast on this page was published before its match kicked off; the scoreboard below settles who was right. Version 2 data: the full international results history (every match since 1872), real per-match xG for the 2026 games, and a classic Elo forecaster.

Model A · launch blend

Weighted xG Poisson

60% 2026 form, 25% last major, 15% WC 2022. Simple, transparent, no opponent adjustment — its known flaw. Frozen on July 9 and never updated: the control group.

Signal processing / econometrics

Dynamic state-space

A Kalman-filtered Poisson (a dynamic GLM, West & Harrison style). Team strength is a latent state that drifts through time; every international since 2012 updates it, friendlies at half gain. It has watched Morocco's entire decade-long rise — and it's the one model that called France–Morocco a coin flip. It regrets that now.

Demand forecasting

Tournament form

This World Cup only, on xG-blended pseudo-goals (60% xG, 40% goals) from the live match data — the "naive seasonal" benchmark every forecasting textbook warns is embarrassingly hard to beat. Won three of the six knockout rounds on the scoreboard.

Chess, 1960

Elo ratings

Arpad Elo's chess system, computed fresh from the results history with football conventions (K by competition, goal-diff multiplier). Current field, post-semifinals: Spain 2259, Argentina 2250 — #1 and #2 in the world, nine points apart — then France 2156, England 2108. It backtested best (80% accuracy), and it is winning the live scoreboard too.

Economic nowcasting

Club-form prior

Like satellite parking-lot data for GDP: 1,800 club matches from the 2025/26 big-five leagues rate each squad's players' clubs, then tilt the forecast. Still can't see Messi (Inter Miami isn't big-five data) — and it has paid for that blindness on the scoreboard.

Methodology, caveats & sources

How This Was Built

Local data. Team and player profiles come from StatsBomb open event data: 123 matches involving these eight teams across WC 2018, WC 2022, Euro 2020, Euro 2024, Copa América 2024, and AFCON 2023 — every pass, shot, pressure, and carry (~3,400 events per match). Historical head-to-heads and records come from the Fjelstul World Cup Database (all 964 men's matches, 1930–2022). 2026 tournament results, form, and injuries were compiled July 9, 2026 from public reporting; knockout results, xG, and Final team news were folded in on July 18 (sources below).

Model A (launch). Each team's attack/defense rating blends 2026 per-match xG and xGA (60%), their most recent major tournament from event data (25%), and WC 2022 (15%). Ratings produce Poisson goal expectancies per match; draws in the knockout resolve by a regulation-strength-weighted coin flip standing in for extra time and penalties. The bracket runs 100,000 Monte Carlo iterations. Frozen at launch, deliberately: it serves as the control for the fancier models — and, six matches in, it has picked every winner.

The ensemble (Forecast Lab, v2). Four further models: a dynamic Poisson state-space filter (extended-Kalman updates on latent attack/defense states across every men's international since 2012 — 49,505-match results history, friendlies at half gain, time-gap-scaled process noise); a tournament-form Poisson on xG-blended pseudo-goals (60% xG / 40% goals, per-match xG from the live 2026 dataset, validated against known results); classic football Elo computed from scratch (K by competition, goal-difference multiplier, no snapshot leakage); and a club-form prior fit on 1,800 matches of 2025/26 big-five-league results mapped through each squad's core players. Weights come from a genuine out-of-sample backtest — all models forecast the WC 2022 and Euro 2024 knockouts using only pre-knockout data (Brier: Elo 0.165, form 0.165, dynamic 0.182, coin flip 0.250, n=30).

The scoreboard (added July 18). Every finished knockout match is scored on forecasts refit from data strictly before its date — the dynamic filter, form model, and Elo are rolled back match by match, so no forecast ever sees its own future (results on the same calendar day are excluded both ways; the data has day, not hour, granularity). Knockout results after the datasets' snapshots enter through a manual results table with reported xG, which also now feeds the Elo updates. Brier = (forecast − outcome)² on the advance probability; extra-time and penalty wins count the same as regulation ones, as the forecasts intended.

Honest caveats. This is a toy model built in an afternoon and updated in an evening, not FiveThirtyEight. It doesn't adjust for opponent strength within tournaments, doesn't regress hot finishing, doesn't model red cards (Embolo's changed QF4), game states (Argentina's entire personality), or the difference between a final and any other match. The final forecast treats Sunday as a neutral fixture between two rating vectors; it is also Messi's last World Cup match, in front of 82,500 people, for the largest prize in the sport, and no Poisson process knows what that does to anyone. Where the model and the market disagree, the market is probably right and the model is more fun. Not gambling advice; the author's stake is joy.
